# Research notes: how many words, which chunks, how Mexican Spanish really sounds, and how to learn it

*Prepared 2026-10-25 for the "Learning Spanish with Penguin" course. Learner: English-speaking engineer, strong listener
once he knows how a phrase is written. Leaves for Mexico 2026-10-30, keeps studying afterwards.*

Every factual claim below links to the page it came from. Where sources disagree, the range is given. Where a
number is my own arithmetic or interpolation from a cited table, it is marked *(estimate)*.

---

## TL;DR

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Is "3,000 words = fluent" right for English? | For **following conversation**, roughly yes: ~3,000 word families give ~95–96% of the words in unscripted speech. For **reading** newspapers and novels, no: that takes ~8,000–9,000 families. |
| Spanish lemmas for **~85%** of everyday speech | **≈ 500–1,000** |
| Spanish lemmas for **~90%** | **≈ 800–1,500** |
| Spanish lemmas for **~95%** | **≈ 2,000–4,000** (best single guess for casual conversation: **~3,000**) |
| Spanish lemmas for **~98%** | **≈ 4,000–6,000+** |
| What do 3,000 Spanish lemmas buy? | About **94–97%** of the words in speech (one unknown word every ~17–33 words) but only **~88–90%** of written text (one every ~8–10 words). Enough to follow everyday talk *with context*, not enough to read a newspaper comfortably. |
| What else matters for listening? | **Chunks** (multiword phrases) and **connected-speech rules**: Spanish glues words together, and central Mexican speech reduces *pues → ps*, *entonces → tons*, *para → pa'*. Even educated Mexico City speakers use a reduced *pues* about 60% of the time. |
| How to study | Retrieval practice (testing yourself), spaced over increasing gaps, listen and read together, mark answers on meaning and correct form gently. |

## 1. Vocabulary size and text coverage

### 1.1 What "coverage" means and which thresholds matter

*Lexical coverage* is the percentage of running words (tokens) in a text that a listener or reader knows.
Comprehension depends heavily on it:

- In the reading study Nation summarises (Hu & Nation 2000), **no one** reached adequate comprehension at 80%
  coverage, a small minority did at 90% and 95%, and a regression put the figure for *most* learners at
  **98%** (one unknown word in 50). ([Nation 2006, CMLR 63(1), pp. 61–62](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf))
- For reading, Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski propose two thresholds: a **minimal** one of 95% (about 4,000–5,000
  word families) and an **optimal** one of 98% (about 8,000 families), both counting proper nouns as known.
  ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010, *Reading in a Foreign Language* 22(1)](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/206))
- For **listening**, van Zeeland & Schmitt tested learners directly. Mean scores on a 10-point test were 9.62 at
  100% coverage, 8.22 at 98%, 7.65 at 95% and 7.35 at 90%. They judged **95%** a reasonable target for
  listening, because performance was much more consistent across people above it, and estimated that 95% can be
  reached with **2,000–3,000 word families**. (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013, *Applied Linguistics* 34(4),
  summarised in [Schmitt, Cobb, Horst & Schmitt 2017, *Language Teaching*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE/core-reader))
- The same replication paper warns that the spoken-language figures rest on few studies and that Nation's
  6,000–7,000 for speech may turn out somewhat lower when checked against other corpora.
  ([Schmitt et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE/core-reader))

**Unit matters.** English studies count **word families**: a headword plus its inflections *and* derivations
(*paint, painted, painter, painting*). Spanish frequency studies count **lemmas**: a headword plus its
inflections only (*pintar, pinto, pintamos* together, but *pintura* and *pintor* counted separately). A family is a
bigger unit than a lemma, so "3,000 English families" and "3,000 Spanish lemmas" are not the same amount of
learning. ([Davies 2005, §4](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf);
[Rojo 2025, n. 1](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf))

### 1.2 English: is the "3,000 words" intuition right?

| Source | Material | Size needed | Coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Nation 2006, Table 13](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf) | Unscripted speech (Wellington corpus: talk-back radio and interviews; family and friends' conversation) | 2,000 families | ~89% |
| | | 3,000 families + proper nouns | **96.0–96.5%** |
| | | 6,000–7,000 families + proper nouns | 97.7–98.6% |
| [Nation 2006, Table 12](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf) | Film script (*Shrek*) | 3,000 / 4,000 / 7,000 families + proper nouns | 94.3% / 96.7% / 98.1% |
| [Nation 2006, abstract](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf) | Written text (novels, newspapers) | 8,000–9,000 families | 98% |
| Adolphs & Schmitt 2003, 2004, as summarised in [Nation, *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*, ch. "Vocabulary and listening and speaking"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/learning-vocabulary-in-another-language/vocabulary-and-listening-and-speaking/E1AACDB39B0F72636009BC910FD455C5) | Informal spoken English (CANCODE, 5M words) | ~3,000 families | ~95% |
| [Shahriari & Motamedynia 2022, *TESL Canada Journal* 39(2)](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/view/1603) | 286 TV episodes | Unscripted TV: 2,000 / 4,000 families; scripted TV: 3,000 / 6,000 families | 95% / 98% |

Adolphs & Schmitt's own conclusion was that *more* vocabulary is needed for everyday speech than had been thought:
the old view, from Schonell et al. 1956, was that 2,000 families covered about 99%.
([Nation 2006, p. 62](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf))

**Verdict for English.** "3,000 words" is a fair rule of thumb for **understanding conversation** (≈95–96% coverage,
the listening threshold). It is **not** enough for comfortable reading (98% needs 8,000–9,000 families). For scale,
an average 20-year-old native speaker of American English knows about **42,000 lemmas** (from about 11,100 word
families) plus about 4,200 multiword expressions.
([Brysbaert et al. 2016, *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1116](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4965448))

### 1.3 Spanish: what the corpus studies say

**Davies (2005): the corpus behind *A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*.** This 20-million-word corpus is split
roughly evenly between speech, fiction and non-fiction, from Spain and Latin America, all from the 1970s or later.
Coverage of tokens by the top lemmas:
([Davies 2005, "Vocabulary Range and Text Coverage: Insights from the Forthcoming Routledge Frequency Dictionary of Spanish", Tables 3–4](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf))

| Top lemmas | Oral | Fiction | Non-fiction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–1,000 | **87.8%** | 79.6% | 76.0% |
| 1–2,000 | **92.7%** | 86.1% | 84.0% |
| 1–3,000 | **94.0%** | 89.6% | 88.2% |

Davies also estimates that the ~6,000-word dictionary gives about **85–90% coverage of a typical text**, depending
on register, and that about 7,000 lemmas give 90% coverage of fiction and nearly 8,000 of non-fiction.
([Davies 2005, §5](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)) Spanish lemma coverage turned out roughly
comparable to English word-family coverage (spoken, first 2,000: Spanish 92.7%, English 90.3%), which Davies
attributes to English's larger stock of near-synonyms (*real, royal, regal*).
([Davies 2005, Table 4](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf))

Caveat: Davies' "oral" section is mostly **educated and formal speech**: *Habla Culta* interviews, press
conferences, parliamentary transcripts and plays, not casual chat between friends.
([Davies 2005, Table 2](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)) Casual conversation re-uses basic words
even more, so its coverage at each level is probably a little higher.

**Rojo (2025): newspaper vs. spoken corpora.** Guillermo Rojo (Real Academia Española) compares the CORPES press
corpus (≈184M words) with ESLORA, a corpus of spoken Spanish from Galicia (≈728k words). The table reads
cumulatively (*estimate* in reading the extracted table: the 95% and 98% points match the author's text):
([Rojo 2025, "Un breve apunte sobre las frecuencias léxicas", Table 1](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf))

| Top lemmas | ESLORA (speech) | CORPES (press) |
|---|---|---|
| 500 | 88.7% | 71.4% |
| 1,000 | 92.8% | 78.9% |
| 2,000 | **96.0%** | 86.2% |
| 3,000 | **97.4%** | 90.0% |
| 4,000 | **98.3%** | 92.4% |
| 6,000 | 99.1% | 95.0% |

Rojo's text says ESLORA needs **2,000 lemmas for 95% and 4,000 for 98%**. For press text, 95% needs about 6,000 and
98% about 12,500. He warns that the corpora differ a lot in size and make-up.
([Rojo 2025, p. 3](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf))
Rojo also points out that these numbers flatter comprehension. Function words (*de, que, el, y*) supply about half
of all tokens, so if you count only content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs), 98% of ESLORA needs about
**5,000** content lemmas. ([Rojo 2025](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf))

**Why the two Spanish sources disagree.** Both rank lemmas *by frequency inside the corpus being measured*. Nation
shows this always gives higher coverage than applying an external list to new material. Your real "top 3,000" will
not match the top 3,000 of tomorrow's conversation exactly.
([Nation 2006, p. 62](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)) The
effect is strongest in a small corpus like ESLORA, so ESLORA is an optimistic bound and Davies (20M words, more
formal speech) a cautious one.

**Subtitles.** SUBTLEX-ESP is a frequency list built from ~41M words of film and TV subtitles. Its frequencies
predict how fast native speakers recognise words better than written-text frequencies do, which makes it a good
proxy for everyday spoken Spanish. It counts word forms, not lemmas, and does not publish coverage curves.
([Cuetos, Glez-Nosti, Barbón & Brysbaert 2011, *Psicológica* 32](https://www.uv.es/revispsi/articulos2.11/1CUETOS.pdf))

### 1.4 Verdict: Spanish lemmas needed for everyday conversation

| Coverage of everyday speech | One unknown word every … | Spanish lemmas | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~85% | 7 words | **≈ 500–1,000** | ESLORA 500 → 88.7%; Davies 1,000 → 87.8% |
| ~90% | 10 words | **≈ 800–1,500** | ESLORA 1,000 → 92.8%; Davies 1,000 → 87.8% and 2,000 → 92.7% *(interpolation)* |
| ~95% (listening threshold) | 20 words | **≈ 2,000–4,000; best guess ~3,000** | ESLORA 2,000 → 96.0%; Davies 3,000 → 94.0% (~4,000 by extrapolation) |
| ~98% (comfortable) | 50 words | **≈ 4,000–6,000+** | ESLORA 4,000 → 98.3%; English speech 6,000–7,000 families |

### 1.5 What "3,000 words" realistically buys in Spanish

- **Conversation, service encounters, TV dialogue:** ~94–97% coverage, at or just above the 95% listening threshold.
  You will follow the thread, and context fills most gaps, but expect an unknown word every couple of sentences.
  ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf);
  [Rojo 2025](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf))
- **Novels, news, signs with specialised terms:** ~88–90%, below the 95% *minimum* for reading. Reading will need a
  dictionary or glosses. ([Davies 2005, Table 3](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf);
  [Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/206))
- **Bonus for this learner:** many lower-frequency Spanish words are Latinate cognates of educated English
  vocabulary. In one study, half the items on an academic spoken English word list were Spanish–English cognates,
  and under 1% were false cognates. So an engineer's real coverage beyond the first 3,000 is higher than the tables
  suggest. ([Lubliner & Hiebert 2011, *Bilingual Research Journal* 34](https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Lubliner-Hiebert.pdf);
  cognate share of the Academic Spoken Word List from [MMU e-space record](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/640667))
- **Coverage is not comprehension.** Proper nouns, accent, speed and *connected speech* (§3) all sit on top of
  word knowledge. Nation also notes that speech, being fleeting, may need *more* coverage than reading, not less.
  ([Nation 2006, p. 77](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf))

## 2. Formulaic language (chunks)

### 2.1 Evidence that chunks matter

- **Language runs on prefabricated phrases.** Sinclair's "idiom principle" holds that speakers choose
  semi-preconstructed phrases as single units. Erman & Warren (2000, *Text* 20(1)) estimated that prefabricated
  combinations make up **roughly half** of English text. Secondary reports give 52–59%, with speech higher than
  writing. Other studies range from ~32% to ~80% depending on how a "chunk" is defined. (Secondary summaries:
  [Diessel, *Grammar and Language Use* lecture notes](https://www.holgerdiessel.uni-jena.de/Grammar%20and%20Language%20Use%204.pdf);
  [*How Chunky is Language?*](https://www.academia.edu/34357207/How_Chunky_is_Language_pdf). I could not read the
  original, so treat "about half" as the safe claim.)
- **Wray's model of the lexicon** treats much of what we store as whole multiword units, not words plus grammar.
  ([Wray 2002, *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon*, CUP](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/3749/))
- **Chunks are processed faster.** Formulaic sequences were read faster than matched non-formulaic phrases by both
  native and non-native readers. ([Conklin & Schmitt 2008, *Applied Linguistics* 29(1)](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/3208445/formulaic-sequences-are-they-processed-more-quickly-than-nonformulaic-language-by-native-and-nonnative-speakers))
  Later reviews note that the advantage is clearest in **proficient** learners. Less proficient learners often still
  read chunks word by word, which is an argument for drilling chunks explicitly.
  ([Conklin & Schmitt 2012, *ARAL*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/processing-of-formulaic-language/8728FEDCAB7A9A280B6EF1CD899B94B1))
- **Chunks make learners sound more fluent.** Boers et al. (2006) reported that learners taught with attention to
  formulaic sequences were judged more proficient in speaking. A 38-hour Chilean follow-up found the same direction
  (higher oral-proficiency scores and more formulaic sequences used). I read only the abstracts, not the 2006 full
  text.
  ([Boers, Eyckmans, Kappel, Stengers & Demecheleer 2006, *Language Teaching Research* 10(3)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/replication-research-in-pedagogical-approaches-to-spoken-fluency-and-formulaic-sequences-a-call-for-replication-of-wood-2009-and-boers-eyckmans-kappel-stengers-demecheleer-2006/8D7676F9CA677D380F6B3870CE20DA9F);
  [Cancino & Iturrieta 2022, *VIAL*](https://revistas.uvigo.es/index.php/vial/article/view/3759))
- **Explicit teaching works better than hoping learners notice.** Boers & Lindstromberg's review of intervention
  studies finds that using formulaic sequences is linked to fluency, and that leaving their acquisition to
  incidental noticing has generally not worked.
  ([Boers & Lindstromberg 2012, *ARAL* 32](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/experimental-and-intervention-studies-on-formulaic-sequences-in-a-second-language/A2ACDF54604CFAC4443240748360C403);
  summary in [TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1271/1106))
- **Word lists miss chunks.** Martinez & Schmitt built the PHRASE List because frequency lists of single words leave
  out common multiword items made of high-frequency words, phrases learners think they understand but don't.
  ([Martinez & Schmitt 2012, *Applied Linguistics* 33(3)](https://scinapse.io/papers/2095682275);
  [Text Inspector description](https://textinspector.com/help/analysing-vocabulary-using-the-phrase-list/))
- **Listening specifically.** In paused-transcription studies, formulaic language helped L2 listeners with
  lower-level decoding, more so on harder texts. But formulas are also the parts of speech most likely to be
  **phonetically reduced**, so they must be heard in reduced form to be recognised.
  ([Language Teaching Research 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168818787828);
  [HKU study](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/220503))
- **Spanish has its own bundle inventory.** Spanish speech and writing have characteristic recurrent multiword
  bundles. ([Tracy-Ventura, Cortes & Biber 2007, in *Working with Spanish Corpora*](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/199141))

### 2.2 Implications for the course

1. Teach chunks **as items in their own right**, with their own cards, audio and SRS schedule, not only as example
   sentences.
2. Prioritise three kinds of chunk:
   - **Survival frames with a slot:** *¿Me da ___?*, *¿Dónde está ___?*, *Quisiera ___*.
   - **High-frequency social formulas:** *¿Qué onda?*, *Mucho gusto*, *Con permiso*, *¿Mande?*
   - **Discourse markers and fillers**, which are the most frequent and most reduced: *pues*, *entonces*, *o sea*,
     *este*, *¿no?*, *a ver*, *la verdad*.
3. Always present a chunk in **careful** and **fast/reduced** audio, with the respelling (§3).
4. Size: native English speakers know roughly one opaque multiword expression per 10 lemmas
   ([Brysbaert et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4965448)). For a 3,000-lemma learner that
   suggests **~300–600 chunks** *(estimate)*, front-loaded with travel needs.

## 3. Connected speech in Spanish, especially Mexican Spanish

The learner understands speech once he has seen it written. The gap is therefore **mapping fast sound to known
spelling**. The rules below are ranked by how often they will trip up a listener in central Mexico (Mexico City and
the central highlands).

### 3.1 Ranked list for Mexico

| # | Rule | What happens | Examples (written → heard) | Mexico notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Resyllabification (enlace)** | A word-final consonant becomes the first sound of the next word if that word starts with a vowel. Word boundaries vanish. | *los otros* → **lo-so-tros**; *las alas* ≈ *la salas*; *es así* → **e-sa-sí**; *un amigo* → **u-na-mi-go** | Universal in Spanish and constant at any speed. English speakers must learn to activate vowel-initial words that are hidden behind a consonant. |
| 2 | **Vowel linking (sinalefa) and merger** | Vowels across a word boundary merge into one syllable; identical vowels fuse. | *lo que es* → **lo kes**; *va a ir* → **vair**; *me encanta* → **men-kan-ta**; *de español* → **des-pa-ñol**; *mi amigo* → **mya-mi-go** | Constant. In fast speech, hiatus inside words also becomes a diphthong (*poeta*, *maestro*). |
| 3 | **Reduced high-frequency words and markers** | The commonest words lose syllables. | *pues* → **pus / ps**; *entonces* → **tonces / entons / tons**; *para* → **pa'** (*pa' que*, *pa'l centro*) | Even **educated** Mexico City speakers used full *pues* only **39.5%** of the time (*pus* 28.7%, *ps* 30%, *pos* 1.8%, n = 600) and full *entonces* 50.5% (*tonces* 22.6%, *entons* 10.9%, *tons* 15.9%, n = 283). *Pa'* occurs in Mexico City but less than in Caracas, and more in lower-class speech. |
| 4 | **Central-Mexican unstressed-vowel weakening** | Unstressed vowels are shortened, devoiced or dropped, especially next to /s/ and before a pause. /a/ resists. | *trastes* → **[trasts]**; *pues* → **ps**; *necesito* → **nes'sito**; *gracias* with a whispered final vowel | A hallmark of the highland plateau of central Mexico. Its gradient: often partial devoicing, not full deletion. |
| 5 | **Weakening or loss of /d/** | /d/ between vowels softens (always, to a light "th"-like sound) and may drop in casual speech. Final /d/ often drops. | *cansado* → **cansao**; *todo* → **to'o**; *nada* → **na'**; *verdad* → **verdá**; *usted* → **usté** | Mexican Spanish weakens consonants **less** than Spain or the Caribbean, so *-ao* is informal and less frequent than in Madrid. Final-/d/ loss is more common among men, in informal style, and with less education. |
| 6 | **Discourse markers you must recognise as single units** | Fillers and markers carry the conversation. | **este…** (= "um"); **o sea** (= "I mean"); **¿mande?** (= "sorry?/yes?"); **ahorita** ("in a bit", not necessarily "right now"); **órale**; **¿qué onda?**; **güey** | *Este* is the most common hesitation marker in Mexico City. *Ahorita* is a softened time word whose "right now" meaning keeps fading among younger speakers. |
| 7 | **/s/ aspiration or loss (regional)** | Syllable-final /s/ becomes [h] or disappears. | *los dos* → **loh doh**; *¿cómo estás?* → **¿cómo ehtá?** | **Not** typical of Mexico City or the highlands, which keep /s/ strongly. **Very frequent on the Pacific and Gulf/Caribbean coasts**, fairly frequent in the north/northwest and parts of Oaxaca and Yucatán. Teach it only as a "you may hear this on the coast" note. |

Examples are illustrative respellings unless a source below gives them (*trastes*, the *pues/entonces* variants,
*pa'*).

  *¿Mande?*, *órale*, *¿qué onda?*, *güey*, *este*: [Wikipedia, "Mexican Spanish"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish).
  *O sea* as the most frequent reformulation marker in conversation:
  [*Beoiberística* 2024](https://beoiberistica.fil.bg.ac.rs/index.php/beoiberistica/en/article/view/beoiber-2024-8-1-2);
  native-speaker preference for *o sea*: [Roberts 2019, PhD, University of Georgia](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/13936).
- Rule 7. [Wikipedia, "Mexican Spanish"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish) (/s/ kept in the interior;
  weakening very frequent on the Pacific and Caribbean coasts; a social marker).

### 3.2 Other things a listener should expect (lower priority)

- **ll = y (yeísmo)** and **z/ce/ci = s (seseo)** everywhere in Mexico: *calló = cayó*, *casa = caza*.
  ([Wikipedia, "Mexican Spanish"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish))
- **j/g(e,i) = velar [x]** in most of Mexico, glottal [h] in some southern areas. **x in place names** varies:
  *México* [x], *Xola* [ʃ]. ([Wikipedia, "Mexican Spanish"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish))
- **Nasal assimilation across words**: *un poco* → **um poco**, *en Guadalajara* → **eŋ Guadalajara**.
  ([Wikipedia, "Spanish phonology"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_phonology))
- **Soft b/d/g between vowels** (*lado*, *haber*, *lago*): a learner listening for hard English stops will miss
  them. ([Wikipedia, "Spanish phonology"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_phonology))
- **Speed.** Spanish is spoken at one of the highest syllable rates measured. Japanese and Spanish were the fastest
  of seven languages in Pellegrino et al. (2011), though each syllable carries less information.
  ([Language Log summary](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=22)) The fix is practice at full speed, not
  waiting for people to slow down.

## 4. Learning science for this learner

### 4.1 Retrieval practice

In Karpicke & Roediger's Swahili–English study, students who kept being **tested** on all 40 word pairs recalled
**about 80%** a week later. Students who kept *studying* but stopped being tested on pairs they had recalled once
recalled only **36%** (and 33%). Repeated study after one successful recall added **nothing** measurable, and
students could not predict this: every group expected about 50%.
([Karpicke & Roediger 2008, *Science* 319:966](https://www.brucehayes.org/Teaching/papers/2008_Roediger_Karpicke_Science.pdf);
[Purdue news release](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html))

**Failing to retrieve still helps**, provided the answer is shown right away. Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhanced
later learning, but with *delayed* feedback the benefit disappeared.
([Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009, *JEP:LMC* 35(4)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/);
[Hays, Kornell & Bjork 2012](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Hays_Kornell_RBjork_inpress.pdf))
→ Ask first, reveal immediately, and never punish a wrong guess.

### 4.2 Spacing

In a study of over 1,350 people, the best gap between study sessions grew with how long the material had to be
remembered: about **20–40% of the retention interval** for a one-week test, falling to **5–10%** for a one-year test.
Cramming into one session was "highly inefficient" for long-term retention.
([Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/);
[PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf))

Implications:
- **Before the trip** (needed in 5–10 days), short gaps of hours to a day are fine. Cramming is acceptable *for the
  trip* but must be followed up with spaced reviews to last.
- **After the trip** (goal: years), use expanding intervals of days, then weeks, then months. This is what standard
  SRS does.
- **Review load scales with new items.** The Anki manual's rule of thumb is that **20 new cards a day leads to about
  200 reviews a day** at steady state. ([Anki manual, Deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/manual/deck-options))
- **Semantic sets.** Learning many near-synonyms or a whole category at once (all colours, all body parts) can cause
  interference. The evidence is mixed, and spacing helps reduce the effect. Mix topics within a session.
  ([Nakata & Suzuki 2019, *SSLA* 41](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10))

### 4.3 Receptive vs productive practice

- Receptive vocabulary (recognising) is larger than productive (producing).
  ([Webb 2008, *SSLA*](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/receptive-and-productive-vocabulary-sizes-of-l2-learners/603A8FD24DBAA2874163DD36D8BAACA3))
- In flashcard research, recognition formats (multiple choice) tend to build form–meaning links efficiently.
  Productive recall (typing the Spanish) is harder during learning but may help spelling and production. The best
  format depends on the goal.
  ([Nakata 2016, *IRAL*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en);
  [Nakata 2013, dissertation summary](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797))
- Word cards used deliberately, with retrieval and spacing, give relatively large gains.
  ([Research synthesis 2022, *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9485613/))

→ For a listener heading to Mexico, the **main card is receptive**: hear Spanish (fast), recall the meaning. Add
**productive typing** for the ~500 survival words and chunks he must *say*.

### 4.4 Reading while listening

- Learners who **read and listened** to 10 graded readers gained **44%** of target words immediately and **37%**
  three months later. ([Webb & Chang 2015, *Language Teaching Research*, "…Extensive Reading With Audio Support"](https://uwo.scholaris.ca/handle/20.500.14721/14630))
- Several studies find an advantage for reading-while-listening over reading alone, probably because it links the
  written and spoken form of each word. This is exactly this learner's gap. Results are moderated by L1 and
  individual differences, and some studies find no difference.
  ([*SSLA* 2024, L1 effects on bimodal input](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/9BCAE9803013AC2323290FE7DA716F46/S0272263124000275a.pdf/first_language_effects_on_incidental_vocabulary_learning_through_bimodal_input.pdf))
- Chang & Millett report "hard evidence" that extensive listening to audio graded readers, with the text available,
  develops L2 listening fluency.
  ([Chang & Millett 2014, *ELT Journal* 68(1), 31–40](https://academic.oup.com/eltj/issue/68/1))

→ Each item should play **careful audio with the text**, then **fast audio with the respelling**, then **fast audio
alone**.

### 4.5 Lenient, meaning-first feedback

- Corrective feedback has significant and durable effects on L2 development.
  ([Lyster & Saito 2010, *SSLA* 32, meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1))
  Earlier syntheses found medium-to-large effects (d ≈ 0.71 immediate, 1.09 delayed).
  ([Mackey & Goo, as cited in Lyster & Saito](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b))
- Nation's "four strands" model argues for roughly equal time on meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output,
  language-focused learning, and fluency development.
  ([Nation & Yamamoto, "Applying the four strands"](https://vuw-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/yamamoto-four-strands.pdf))
- Errors made during learning do not hurt when they are corrected right away (see 4.1).

→ Score **recall of meaning**. Show grammar slips (gender, agreement, verb ending) **with a one-line correction**,
and do not fail the item for them. This matches the contract's grading philosophy and the evidence that feedback
helps without making each answer pass/fail on form.

## 5. A realistic plan

### 5.1 Five days before the trip (Sun 25 – Thu 29 Oct 2026)

Goal: **survive and connect**, not coverage. At 60–90 min/day:

| Day | New items | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~40 | Greetings and courtesy chunks (*¿mande?*, *con permiso*, *disculpe*), numbers 1–20, top function words (*de, que, el/la, y, en, un, ser, estar, tener, ir*). Sound rules 1–2 (enlace, sinalefa). |
| 2 | ~40 | Ordering food and drink: *¿Me da…?*, *la cuenta*, *para llevar*, numbers 20–100, prices. Rule 3 (*pa'*, *pues → ps*). |
| 3 | ~40 | Directions, transport, hotel: *¿Dónde está…?*, *a la derecha*, *derecho*, *cuadra*, *camión*, *boleto*, *cuarto*. Rule 5 (*-ado → -ao*, *to'o*). |
| 4 | ~40 | Problems and emergencies, shopping, time (*ahorita* = "in a bit"), "I don't understand / slower please". Rule 4 (vowel devoicing). Fillers: *este, o sea, ¿no?* |
| 5 | ~20 + review | Review everything with fast audio only, plus a story prompt (landing → taxi → hotel → tacos). |

Total: **~180 items** *(estimate)* (≈100 chunks + ≈80 lemmas). On a ~1-week horizon, gaps of hours to a day are
near-optimal ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)), so 2–3 short sessions a day beat one
long one.

### 5.2 During and after the trip (Nov 2026 onward)

- **New items:** 15–25 a day. At 20/day, 3,000 lemmas + ~500 chunks ≈ **6 months** *(estimate)*. Speed up the
  transparent cognates (*hospital, problema, posible*) as recognition-only cards.
- **Reviews:** expect **~10× the daily new load** once it settles (about 200/day at 20 new/day,
  [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/manual/deck-options)). Cap reviews and reduce new items if a backlog builds.
- **Milestones** *(estimates from §1.4)*:
  - ~1,000 lemmas (≈ 88–93% of speech): about 6–7 weeks.
  - ~2,000 (≈ 93–96%): about 3 months.
  - ~3,000 (≈ 94–97%): about 5–6 months.
- **Every day, 10–15 min of meaning-focused listening with text** (reading-while-listening, captioned Mexican TV
  or YouTube) on top of SRS.

## Recommendations for the course

1. **Target size: 3,000 lemmas**, ordered by spoken frequency, which gives ~94–97% coverage of everyday speech
   (the listening threshold). Plan a stretch tier to 5,000 later for reading. Say plainly in the app that 3,000 =
   conversational, not newspaper-level.
   ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf);
   [Rojo 2025](https://gramatica.usc.es/~grojo/Publicaciones/Rojo_Apunte_sobre_frecuencias_lexicas.pdf);
   [van Zeeland & Schmitt via Schmitt et al. 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE/core-reader))
2. **Chunks: ~500 (range 300–600)**, as first-class SRS items:
   - ~150 travel/survival frames and formulas, front-loaded.
   - ~150 discourse markers, fillers and social formulas (*pues, entonces, o sea, este, ¿no?, a ver, ¿mande?,
     ahorita, órale, ¿qué onda?*).
   - ~200 high-frequency collocations and bundles (*a lo mejor, lo que pasa es que, tener que, ir a, hace falta,
     de repente, por eso*).
3. **Ordering: survival first, then frequency.**
   - Units 1–2 (the 5 pre-trip days): ~100 survival chunks + top ~80–100 lemmas, mostly function words.
   - From then on, **strict frequency order** in batches of ~10, each batch padded with 1–2 topic words that make
     its example sentences usable.
   - Avoid whole semantic sets in one lesson (e.g., all 12 months at once): spread them out
     ([Nakata & Suzuki 2019](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10)).
4. **Daily load:**
   - Pre-trip: **~40 new items/day** (≈8–12 per lesson, 3–4 lessons) with same-day re-tests.
   - Afterwards: **15–25 new/day** (default 20), reviews capped at ~200/day, new items paused automatically when the
     due backlog exceeds ~1.5× the cap.
   - Treat transparent cognates as quick recognition cards.
5. **Card design:**
   - Retrieval first, immediate feedback. Primary card: **hear (fast) → meaning**.
   - Secondary: **read → meaning**, and **type Spanish** for the survival set and chunks.
   - Every item gets careful audio + text, then natural-speed audio + respelling, then audio-only.
     ([Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://www.brucehayes.org/Teaching/papers/2008_Roediger_Karpicke_Science.pdf);
     [Webb & Chang 2015](https://uwo.scholaris.ca/handle/20.500.14721/14630))
6. **Grading:** score meaning. Accents, typos and grammar slips get a kind one-line note, not a fail. Show the
   correct form immediately after every attempt
   ([Kornell et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/);
   [Lyster & Saito 2010](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)).
7. **Connected-speech rules, in teaching order for Mexico:**
   1. **Enlace / resyllabification:** *los otros → lo-so-tros*.
   2. **Sinalefa / vowel merging:** *lo que es → lo kes*, *va a ir → vair*.
   3. **Reduced function words and markers:** *pues → pus/ps*, *entonces → tons*, *para → pa'*. Heard in most
      Mexico City *pues* tokens.
   4. **Central-Mexican unstressed-vowel devoicing**, especially near *s*: *trastes → trasts*, *pues → ps*.
   5. **/d/ weakening:** *-ado → -ao*, *todo → to'o*, *verdad → verdá*. Present it as casual and less common than
      in Spain.
   6. **Discourse markers as sound-units:** *este…*, *o sea*, *¿mande?*, *ahorita*, *órale*, *¿qué onda?*
   7. **Background notes:** nasal assimilation (*un poco → um poco*), soft b/d/g, yeísmo/seseo.
   8. **Coastal /s/ aspiration** (*los dos → loh doh*): an optional "if you go to the coast" note.
8. **Daily habit outside SRS:** 10–15 min of reading-while-listening or captioned Mexican video. It is the most
   direct fix for this learner's specific gap between spelling and sound.

### Notes on sources and limits

- The Spanish 95%/98% figures come from two corpus studies (Davies 2005; Rojo 2025) that rank lemmas inside the
  corpus being measured. Real-world coverage of a new conversation will be somewhat lower than the tables show.
- No published study I found gives lemma-based coverage curves for **Mexican** conversation or Spanish subtitles.
  The ranges above are the honest best estimate.
- The connected-speech rates are from specific corpora (e.g., 1960s–70s educated Mexico City speech for
  *pues/entonces*). Casual and younger speech probably reduces as much or more, but that is not measured here.
